No. "Always goes up after a halving" isn't an established rule, and putting all your savings into one volatile asset is risky whatever the halving pattern turns out to be.

**What's known:** There have been four halvings: 2012, 2016, 2020 and April 2024. After each of the first three, the price rose a lot within roughly the following year or two. Each of those rises then ended in a crash. From memory (check before relying on these numbers), the falls from peak were around 75–85%.

**What's inferred:** People say "halving causes the rise" because fewer new coins come in while demand stays the same. That's a reasonable theory, but four events is far too few to prove it. Those rallies also lined up with other things, like low interest rates and new money flowing into crypto. So we can't separate the halving's effect from everything else happening at the time.

**What's unknown:** Whether the pattern still holds now that ETFs and big institutions are in the market. Also, the last halving was about two and a half years ago, so "now" isn't really "right after a halving." The next one is expected around 2028. I don't have today's price, so I can't tell you where we are in any cycle.

**The strongest case for buying:** Supply is fixed and keeps getting scarcer, and the past pattern did happen. A small position you can afford to lose and hold for years is a defensible choice.

**The case against going all in:** Even if the pattern repeats, history says you could be down 75% or more at some point along the way. If that money is your emergency fund or something you'll need soon, you could be forced to sell at the bottom.

**My confidence:** HIGH that putting all your savings in is a mistake. That rests on how deep Bitcoin's past drawdowns have been and on basic diversification. LOW on where the price goes next. Nobody can know that from four data points.

**What would change my view:** Only your situation, not the price. For example, if this is truly spare money beyond an emergency fund (often suggested as 3–6 months of expenses, which is a rule of thumb) and you could watch it fall by most of its value without selling. Even then, size it as a slice of your savings, not all of them.
