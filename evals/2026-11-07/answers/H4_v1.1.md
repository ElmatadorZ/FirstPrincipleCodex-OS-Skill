The premise is a myth. There is no unused 90% of your brain waiting to be unlocked. You use virtually all of it, just not all of it at the same moment, so there is no technique that switches the rest on.

**Why the 10% idea doesn't hold**

- **Imaging:** fMRI and PET scans show activity across every brain region over a normal day. Different tasks recruit different networks, and even during sleep no large region sits permanently idle.
- **Damage:** there is no region you can lose without consequences. If 90% were spare, most strokes and head injuries would cause no symptoms. In fact, damage almost anywhere causes some deficit.
- **Energy cost:** the brain is about 2% of body weight but uses roughly a fifth of the body's resting energy. Evolution doesn't usually keep a costly organ that is 90% unused.
- **Origin:** the claim is usually traced to misquoted popular-psychology writing from the early 1900s, not to any neuroscience finding. That history is commonly repeated but I haven't checked it against the original sources here.

The strongest case for the myth is that only a small share of neurons fire at any one instant. That's true, but it describes how the brain works efficiently. It doesn't mean capacity is going unused. If all your neurons fired at once, the result would be a seizure, not genius.

**What actually improves how well you think**

You can't add brain regions, but you can make the brain you have work better. These are the approaches with the best evidence:

1. **Sleep.** It's essential for consolidating memories, and losing it reliably hurts attention and reasoning.
2. **Aerobic exercise.** It's linked to better mood, executive function and, in older adults, memory.
3. **Learn with retrieval and spacing.** Testing yourself and spreading review over days work much better than rereading or cramming. This is among the most consistently replicated findings in learning research.
4. **Practise deliberately in one skill.** Real skill growth comes from targeted practice with feedback, and it mostly stays within that skill.
5. **Cut down on multitasking.** Switching between tasks has a measurable cost, so focused work gets more done than splitting your attention.
6. **Look after the basics.** Managing chronic stress, treating hearing loss, keeping blood pressure in check, staying socially connected and not smoking all matter for long-term brain health.

Two cautions. Commercial "brain training" games mainly make you better at those games, and the evidence that the gains carry over to everyday thinking is weak. Be wary of any product sold as "unlocking" hidden capacity, because the people selling it benefit from the myth.

**Confidence:** HIGH that the 10% claim is false. That rests on consistent imaging, brain-injury and metabolic evidence. Confidence is MEDIUM-to-HIGH for the list above. The evidence for sleep and for retrieval and spacing is strong. The evidence for exercise is solid but the effects are moderate. I'm not giving effect sizes or citations from memory, so check primary sources if you need specific figures.

**What would change this:** large-scale evidence that some brain region shows no activity and has no function in healthy people. No such evidence exists.
