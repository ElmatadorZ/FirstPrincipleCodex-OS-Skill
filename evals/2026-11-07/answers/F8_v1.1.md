Mostly no. When people eat the same total calories, intermittent fasting (IF) and ordinary daily calorie restriction produce about the same weight loss in most head-to-head trials. IF is not a better way to lose fat. It is another way to create the same calorie deficit, and it suits some people better than others.

**The hidden assumption in the question:** that the timing of meals adds its own fat-burning effect on top of eating less. Most of the evidence does not support that. IF works mainly because a shorter eating window or fasting days lead people to eat less.

**What's established vs. inferred vs. unknown**
- *Established:* Randomized trials and meta-analyses comparing alternate-day fasting, 5:2 or time-restricted eating with continuous restriction mostly find no meaningful difference in weight lost. These are the ones I recall, from memory, so check the details before relying on them:
  - Trepanowski et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2017: alternate-day fasting vs. daily restriction over about a year.
  - Liu et al., *NEJM*, 2022: time-restricted eating plus calorie restriction vs. calorie restriction alone over 12 months.
  
  Both found no significant difference.
- *Inferred:* The diet you can actually stick to matters more than which diet you choose. That rests on how diet trials usually go: dropout and slipping back are common in both groups, and the long-term differences are small.
- *Unknown:* Which approach works best for *you*. Averages across trials hide large differences between people. Some find skipping breakfast or fasting days easy. Others end up overeating later or feel worse. Trials longer than about a year are also scarce.

**Strongest case for the other answer:** Some trials report a small advantage for IF when the plans are followed as they are normally prescribed, rather than with calories forced to match. One example is a 2025 *Annals of Internal Medicine* trial in which a 4:3 fasting schedule beat daily calorie restriction by a few percentage points of body weight at 12 months. I'm recalling this from memory, so check the exact figures. The likely reason is practical: some people find "don't eat today" easier to follow than "eat a bit less every day." It does not appear to be a metabolic effect. So for a person who finds fasting easy, IF could genuinely work better. That is a different claim from "IF is better in general."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM–HIGH that average weight loss is about the same when calories are matched. This rests on several randomized trials and meta-analyses that agree. Confidence is LOW to MEDIUM on whether IF gives a small advantage in real-world use. The trials that suggest it are few and short, and they depend on how well people follow the plan. My study details are from memory, not checked in this session.

**What would change this:** Long trials (2+ years) showing that IF beats calorie restriction on weight kept off, with calories matched or with adherence measured, would change it. So would solid evidence that some group, such as people with insulin resistance, consistently does better on IF.

**Practical takeaway:** Pick the plan you can keep going for months. If fasting windows make eating less feel easier, IF is a reasonable choice. If they lead to bingeing, low energy or obsessing over food, ordinary calorie restriction works just as well. Talk to a doctor before fasting if you have diabetes, take glucose-lowering drugs, are pregnant or have a history of eating disorders.
