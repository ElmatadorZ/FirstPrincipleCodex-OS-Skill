📍 REALITY ANCHOR
Known: The "we only use 10% of our brains" claim is a myth. Brain imaging (fMRI, PET) shows activity across practically every region over a normal day, even during sleep. Damage to almost any area causes some deficit. The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of resting energy, and an organ that costly would not keep 90% of its tissue idle. | Inferred: The myth probably grew from misquoted early psychology (William James wrote that people use only a small part of their *potential*) and from early neuroscience, when the function of many regions was not yet mapped. | Unknown: Nothing unknown changes the core answer. The open questions are only about how much real-world gain each technique below gives *you*.

⚡ ABSOLUTISM: "only 10%". This is a precise number with no evidence chain behind it, so it fails Kalama10 #1 (rumor) and #9 (consensus with no traceable evidence).

📍 SYNTHESIS
**You already use all of your brain.** The 10% figure is a myth, so there is no hidden 90% to unlock. What you *can* do is train and maintain the brain you have so it works better. Here is what has the strongest evidence, in order of leverage:

1. **Sleep 7–9 hours.** During sleep the brain consolidates memories and clears metabolic waste. Short sleep reliably harms attention, memory, and judgement. This is the biggest and cheapest gain.
2. **Exercise regularly.** Aerobic exercise (around 150 minutes a week) is linked to better memory and executive function, and it raises BDNF, a protein that supports neural plasticity.
3. **Learn with retrieval practice.** Testing yourself (flashcards, recalling material from memory) beats rereading by a wide margin in learning research.
4. **Use spaced repetition.** Review material at increasing intervals instead of cramming. Tools like Anki automate this.
5. **Practise deliberately.** Work just beyond your current ability, get quick feedback, and fix specific weaknesses. This is how experts build skill.
6. **Single-task.** Multitasking is really rapid switching, and each switch costs accuracy and time. Use blocks of focused work and put the phone in another room.
7. **Manage chronic stress.** Long-term high stress impairs memory and focus. Exercise, sleep, social connection, and techniques such as mindfulness all help, although mindfulness effects on cognition are modest.
8. **Keep learning hard new things.** Languages, instruments, and complex skills build what researchers call "cognitive reserve", which is associated with healthier aging.
9. **Cover the basics.** Treat hearing and vision problems, avoid heavy drinking, stay socially connected, and eat a generally balanced diet.

**What to skip:** "unlock your brain" supplements (evidence for gains in healthy people is weak at best), subliminal audio, and brain-training games sold as general intelligence boosters. Those games mostly make you better at the games themselves.

📍 CONFIDENCE FIELD
CONFIDENCE    : 95% that the 10% claim is false. 80–85% on the ranking of techniques, because individual responses vary and effect sizes differ between studies.
SHADOW VERDICT: CONSISTENT
UNKNOWNS      : Your current sleep, health, stress level, and goals, which decide which technique gives *you* the biggest gain.
FAILURE COND  : An underlying medical issue (such as sleep apnea, depression, thyroid problems, or ADHD) can limit cognition in ways these habits will not fix. If your focus or memory problems are persistent or worsening, see a doctor.
